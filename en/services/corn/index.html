What is actually on offer

Grain maize, FAO 280–340 hybrids. That is a deliberate range: longer-season hybrids give higher yield potential on our fields, but demand a bigger heat-unit budget to reach maturity and a bigger drying load at harvest. We do not push into FAO 400+ for the extra tonnage — the risk of not maturing before frost outweighs the gain.

The 2026 season closed at 9.8 t/ha across the whole area — 33,300 tonnes gross. Maize occupies 3,400 of our 14,200 hectares, the second-largest crop in the rotation after wheat, and on the elevator lots are separated not into one tonnage but by hybrid and by moisture content at intake from the field.

  • FAO 280–340 — the balance between yield potential and guaranteed maturity before frost.
  • Lots are kept separate by hybrid rather than averaged into one silo.
  • Every lot carries a known moisture history from field to dryer.

Why hybrid choice is also a drying decision

Hybrid selection is not only about yield potential — it is sized against our own drying capacity. A longer-FAO hybrid matures later and comes off the field at 22–28% moisture, sometimes higher after a wet autumn. Planting too much of one late hybrid at once means the dryers cannot clear the incoming flow fast enough, and wet grain sits in the intake bins longer than is safe.

So we stagger planting across FAO groups by maturity date: some fields enter harvest up to two weeks ahead of others. That is not about the yield of any one field — it is about keeping the dryers loaded evenly instead of hitting a peak that forces combines to wait.

Combining starts once field moisture has dropped below 30%, not on a calendar date. Threshing wetter grain earlier causes more mechanical kernel damage in the threshing unit, and a damaged kernel is both an entry point for mould in storage and a direct source of broken grain in the lot.

  • Planting dates for different hybrids are staggered to even out the load on the dryers.
  • Combining follows measured field moisture, not a calendar date.
  • Less mechanical kernel damage means less mould risk and fewer broken kernels.

Drying: the real economics of maize

For maize, drying is not a routine finishing step — it is the main cost line and the main way to lose a lot. Grain comes off the field wet, often 22–28% moisture, and every point of moisture the dryer removes costs fuel and dryer-hours. We do not cut corners here: grain put into storage "nearly dry" heats up in the silo within weeks, and no amount of aeration afterwards fixes that.

Drying runs across three units with a combined capacity of 1,200 tonnes per day, and at the peak of maize harvest that throughput is scheduled by the hour to avoid a queue of combines at the intake point. Moisture is removed gradually, without overheating the kernel — a sharp temperature swing on high-moisture grain cracks the pericarp, and that is exactly the defect that later shows up as broken kernels on the buyer's scale.

Moisture is checked at every stage — on intake from the field, on the way out of the dryer, and again before dispatch, because grain can pick moisture back up in storage when temperatures swing. Every lot ships at 14% moisture or below, confirmed by a laboratory certificate rather than a visual reading off the intake moisture meter.

Storage and mycotoxin control

Our own elevator holds 46,000 tonnes; with leased capacity the total is 58,000 tonnes. Maize is stored in silos with active aeration: grain dried to 14% is cooled to storage temperature immediately after the dryer, because a warm, humid air pocket inside a silo is enough to start mould growth even in grain that technically passed on moisture alone.

Mycotoxins — fumonisins and aflatoxins — are managed proactively, not after a buyer complaint. Any lot showing an elevated share of damaged or mould-affected kernels at intake is routed to a separate silo and never blended with a clean lot. We hold aflatoxin B1 at or below 5 µg/kg, a threshold agreed with our regular buyers as the benchmark for export lots — in line with the DSTU 4964 grain-safety requirements and comparable to the EU limit under Regulation (EC) No 1881/2006 for maize destined for further processing.

Ongoing temperature monitoring — sensors placed through the height of the grain column — flags a local hotspot before it becomes a whole-lot problem. If the thermometry shows a deviation, we aerate or transfer that section rather than waiting for a lab result to confirm it.

  • Grain is cooled to storage temperature immediately after drying.
  • Suspect lots go to a dedicated silo, never blended with clean grain.
  • Column thermometry in every silo for early detection of heating.

Logistics and dispatch

The elevator stands in Soroka, Vinnytsia region: 35 km to Haisyn, 85 km to Vinnytsia, 230 km to Kyiv and 380 km to the port of Odesa. Maize moves for export mainly through the port, and that distance is a single road run with no transhipment through an intermediate warehouse.

Dispatch is by road, on your trucks or ours. A lot from 100 tonnes is scheduled for loading within three working days of agreement; during peak season, in October and November, we ask for the loading date to be booked in advance, since the queue for outbound loading runs high. Weighbridges are calibrated and certified, and weighing takes place in the presence of the buyer's representative if they wish to attend.

Commercial terms

We work under contract, VAT registered, with a complete document package. Minimum lot is 100 tonnes. Spot payment is 3–5 banking days after acceptance; on a forward, an advance and a payment schedule are fixed in the contract before sowing, early enough to commit a specific hybrid and area to the contract.

Spot price is the price on the day of dispatch. Forward volume on maize is typically taken for delivery after October, once dryer-confirmed quality is already known. Since 2008 we have shipped grain to buyers in 14 countries, and every lot ships with an elevator laboratory certificate — moisture, foreign matter, test weight, starch — issued before loading, not after.
