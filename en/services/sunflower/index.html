What is actually on offer

Sunflower seed of high-oleic and high-oil hybrids, 46–49% oil content on a dry matter basis. This is a crush-plant commodity, not planting seed: the buyer cares about oil yield per tonne, not germination or varietal purity. Lots from different fields are not commingled before a shipment lot is formed, so the oil content on the certificate is the measured figure for that specific tonnage, not an average across several intakes.

Sunflower occupies 2,600 of our 14,200 hectares — a profitable crop, but a demanding one in rotation: we do not return it to the same field sooner than every four to five years, otherwise both yield and oil content fall as field-specific pathogens build up in the soil. The 2026 season closed at 3.4 t/ha — 8,800 tonnes gross.

Of the seven crops in our rotation, sunflower is the most exacting about following the agronomic calendar precisely, because the cost of a mistake shows up not in the field but at the elevator: an under-dried or over-standing lot loses value faster than any cereal does. That is why our harvest schedule for sunflower is agreed with the drying complex in advance, rather than drying being fitted around whatever volume arrives from the field.

  • High-oil hybrids, selected on yield and oil content together.
  • Lots are formed by field and harvest date, never blended in.
  • Seed for crushing, not for the planting-seed market.

Hybrid selection and why oil content stays stable

We choose a hybrid not on maximum yield alone, but on the product of yield and oil content — that figure determines how much oil a plant physically extracts from our tonne. Crop protection follows the regional standard: sunflower is vulnerable to broomrape and Sclerotinia, so the field for a new planting is chosen with the previous years' history in mind, and we do not plant sunflower after sunflower.

Desiccation is used selectively, only when natural ripening is delayed by weather — it speeds up harvest in a wet, unstable September, but it is not our standard practice on every field. The main quality-control lever at this stage is harvest timing: sunflower left standing too long in the field loses oil content and picks up acid value before the combine ever reaches it.

Scouting for broomrape and head disease is done by our own agronomist field by field, so the decision on a given plot is made on the evidence from that plot, not on an average schedule for the whole farm.

  • Hybrids are selected on the product of yield and oil content.
  • Rotation no more frequent than once every four to five years per field.
  • Harvest timing is set by head condition, not by the calendar.

Laboratory and lot certificate

Analysis of sunflower seed is done in our own laboratory at the elevator. Every truck coming off the field passes intake control for moisture, foreign matter and seed damage. A completed lot then receives a full certificate: oil content, moisture, acid value, free fatty acid content, hull content, peroxide value and thousand-seed weight.

Acid value is our main indicator of how a lot has behaved after harvest, not just in the field. It rises during storage of wet or damaged seed: a broken hull lets moisture and air reach the kernel, enzymes start breaking down the oil, and after a few weeks of poor storage a lot that looked excellent in the field arrives at the plant with an acid value past the acceptance limit. That is why we do not hold sunflower seed wet "until the weekend" — it is dried on the day it comes in.

The buyer receives the certificate before dispatch. If a lot does not meet a given parameter, it stays on the elevator — we do not ship on a "load it and trust the truck" basis.

Drying and storage: why an oilseed is a different discipline

Sunflower seed often arrives for drying at 12–14% moisture or higher and is brought down to a target of 6–8% — noticeably lower than for cereal grain. That is not a margin of safety taken for its own sake: oilseed behaves differently from grain in storage, and every extra percentage point of moisture sharply shortens the time a lot stays stable on acid value.

Drying an oilseed is a distinct fire-risk discipline, not the same regime used for wheat or maize. Sunflower hulls and pollen are combustible and can smoulder inside the mass without visible flame, so drier temperature settings for sunflower are held lower than for cereals, and temperature monitoring through the grain column is continuous, not spot-checked. Three drying units rated at 1,200 tonnes per day let us work carefully rather than push volume at the risk of overheating.

Sunflower is stored apart from cereal crops — in dedicated silos with active aeration and temperature monitoring through the height of the pile. We do not mix an oilseed with cereal grain in the same bin: different bulk density, different storage behaviour and a different self-heating risk make that a false economy even when free silo space is tight.

  • Dried from 12–14% down to a target of 6–8%, a lower setting than for cereals.
  • Continuous temperature monitoring due to hull smouldering risk.
  • Dedicated silos for oilseed, never mixed with cereal grain.

Logistics and dispatch

The elevator stands in Soroka, Vinnytsia region: 35 km to Haisyn, 85 km to Vinnytsia, 230 km to Kyiv and 380 km to the port of Odesa. The main buyers of our sunflower seed are regional crushing plants and port terminals that process seed for oil export, rather than buyers of the raw seed itself.

Dispatch is by road, on your trucks or ours. A lot from 100 tonnes is scheduled for loading within three working days of agreement. Because seed is lighter than grain by volume, the same tonnage needs more trucks — we account for that in the loading schedule in advance so dispatch does not stretch over extra days. Weighbridges are calibrated and certified, and weighing takes place in the presence of the buyer's representative for every truck if they wish to attend.

Commercial terms

We work under contract, VAT registered, with a complete document package. Minimum lot is 100 tonnes. Spot payment is 3–5 banking days after acceptance; on a forward, an advance and a payment schedule are fixed in the contract before sowing.

Spot price is the price on the day of dispatch, and it is usually linked to oil quotations at the time of the deal, not to seed tonnage alone. A forward makes sense for a plant that is scheduling crushing capacity ahead of time: we commit the volume, and you get a predictable delivery. Since 2008 we have shipped seed to buyers in 14 countries, and the paperwork for every lot is prepared by our own accounts department rather than an intermediary.
