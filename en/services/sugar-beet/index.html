Sugar beet

What is actually on offer

Sugar beet of technical hybrids grown under contract with sugar plants in the region. This is not a commodity held for open sale and not a position kept in a warehouse pending a better price — the plant's tonnage is agreed before sowing, and every tonne lifted from the field has an assigned recipient and a reception date. There is no such thing as buying this beet 'off the yard' after harvest: it is contracted ahead of the season, not sourced from storage once it is out of the ground.

400 hectares of beet is the smallest area but the most demanding crop in the rotation by input cost. The 2025 season closed at 58 t/ha — 23,200 tonnes gross. Hybrids are selected for sugar content and resistance to Cercospora leaf spot rather than for maximum root weight: a heavier root is not automatically a sweeter one, and the plant pays for sugar, not for kilograms.

  • The plant contract is fixed before sowing — tonnage, price per unit of sugar, indicative campaign window.
  • Technical-type hybrids selected for sugar content and Cercospora leaf spot resistance.
  • Pelleted seed, 4.5–5.5 mm calibration, treated against beet leaf miner and flea beetle.

Rotation and soil: the most demanding crop

Beet returns to the same field no sooner than every four years — a shorter interval lets nematode and Cercospora pressure build to a level no fungicide programme can offset. The preceding crop is winter cereal: it clears the field early, leaves it clean of weeds, and gives time for deep autumn tillage.

Beet draws more potassium and nitrogen from the soil than any other crop in the rotation, so fertiliser rates are set from a pre-sowing agrochemical analysis of each field, run by our own laboratory, rather than from an average recommendation. Liming is applied where pH drops below 6.0 — on acid soil, beet loses sugar content even at high yield.

Soil compaction is the root's principal enemy: a beet growing in a compacted subsoil layer forks, and a forked root is harder to clean of soil and harder to lift without loss. So on beet fields we limit the number of machinery passes and run wide-tyred tractors for every field operation, including top-dressing.

Crop protection and in-season monitoring

The main threats are beet leaf miner at emergence, Cercospora leaf spot, and root rot in the second half of the growing season. Fields are scouted weekly by our agronomist from the forked-leaf stage through row closure, and every treatment decision is made against an actual damage threshold, not a calendar programme.

Herbicide protection is applied in a band sequence: beet grows slowly through the first six weeks and cannot withstand weed competition during that window, so the first application goes down at emergence rather than waiting until weeds are visible across the field. Fungicide treatment against Cercospora follows the weather forecast and measured canopy humidity, typically two to three applications in the second half of summer.

  • Banded herbicide applied at emergence, ahead of visible weed pressure across the field.
  • Weekly scouting for beet leaf miner and Cercospora leaf spot from emergence to row closure.
  • Fungicide against Cercospora timed to forecast conditions, not a fixed calendar.

Lifting and the campaign clock

Sugar in a lifted root degrades daily — a week in a field pile can cost 0.3–0.5 percentage points of sugar content, and more where the crown is damaged. So lifting is planned not for convenience but against the plant's actual reception slots: beet is lifted for a lot that reaches the plant within a day or two, not stockpiled in the field.

Crown removal is controlled to leaf-scar level: an under-cut crown carries leaf mass with low sugar and raises the lot's soil tare and impurity reading; an over-cut crown loses root mass and opens the root to rot. The harvester and loader work to the haulage schedule so that beet does not sit in a windrow for more than a day.

Haulage runs on our own and contracted trucks, coordinated with the plant's dispatch office: a reception slot is booked in advance, and each truck arrives within its own window rather than joining a general queue. The campaign runs from late September until the plant finishes processing, and the lifting schedule across fields is sequenced so that each field's peak sugar content reaches the plant instead of being lost in a pile.

  • Lifting is scheduled against a specific plant reception slot, not held in a field pile.
  • Crown-cut quality checked to leaf-scar level on every harvesting unit.
  • Haulage books its reception window through the plant's dispatch office in advance.

How sugar content is measured and paid

Sugar content and soil tare are determined by the plant's own laboratory at the reception point — that is the figure payment is calculated on, not a reading taken on our field. Our laboratory runs weekly pre-harvest digestion monitoring across fields from mid-August, to pin down the window of peak sugar accumulation for each field so a lot is never dispatched too early or too late.

Settlement is built on digestion: tonnes of root multiplied by the measured sugar percentage, net of soil tare and any deduction for rotten roots beyond the allowed threshold. Where our pre-delivery reading and the plant laboratory's figure diverge, a duplicate sample is drawn at reception under a right-to-counter-sample clause, and in the event of a dispute it is analysed by an arbitration laboratory named in the contract.

The contract model

We work under a seasonal contract with the sugar plant, signed before sowing, that fixes the area, the indicative tonnage, the price formula per unit of sugar, and the overall campaign window. This is a fundamentally different model from selling grain off an elevator — there is no finished-goods store here and no option to wait for a better price, because beet cannot be held for months.

The plant settles each delivered lot after laboratory analysis at reception, normally through the campaign on the schedule fixed in the contract. VAT registered, full document package. Haulage to the plant is arranged with our own and contracted transport, and the haulage cost is carried as a separate line in the contract price rather than added after the fact.
