What is actually on offer

Soft winter wheat of food grade, class 2 and class 3 under DSTU 3768:2019 — the Ukrainian standard. For a buyer used to other nomenclature, class 2 corresponds to standard milling wheat on a 12.5% protein basis, and class 3 to a feed-and-blend grade from 11%. Flour mills take the first for straight-run milling; exporters take it against protein-specified contracts.

The 2026 season closed at 6.4 t/ha across the whole area — 30,700 tonnes gross. Wheat occupies 4,800 of our 14,200 hectares, which makes it the largest crop in the rotation, and that is precisely why it is stored as separate clusters rather than as one tonnage.

Wheat is sold from our elevator rather than out of the field through an intermediary: the buyer is looking at a specific lot with a specific certificate and can decline it before loading, not after it has been accepted at their own plant.

  • Class 2 — protein from 12.5%, for straight-run milling and export.
  • Class 3 — protein from 11%, for blending into technical formulations.
  • Lots are held by field of origin and are never commingled.

Agronomy behind a stable specification

Wheat follows rapeseed and soybean in our rotation. Both leave structured soil and residual nitrogen, so establishment does not depend on September rainfall the way it does after a cereal. A longer rotation costs us area under the highest-value crop, and buys evenness across fields — and evenness is what a buyer is actually paying for.

Sowing date is set by measured soil moisture, not by the calendar. Nitrogen is applied variably: each field is divided into productivity zones from multi-year yield maps and NDVI imagery, and each zone gets its own rate. On weak zones that saves fertiliser; on strong ones it prevents lodging.

Crop protection follows scouting rather than a preventive calendar programme. Our own agronomist walks the fields, and every treatment decision is recorded with its date and its reason. Those records form part of the lot history and are available on request.

  • Preceding crops: rapeseed and soybean, rotation of four fields or more.
  • Variable-rate nitrogen by productivity zone.
  • Treatments on scouting evidence, with date and rationale recorded.

Laboratory and lot certificate

Analysis is done in our own laboratory at the elevator, not by a contractor called in for the season. Every truck coming off the field passes intake control for moisture, foreign matter, damaged kernels and test weight. A completed lot then receives a full certificate: protein, wet gluten, falling number, test weight and moisture.

We hold falling number at 240 seconds and above. That is the threshold below which baking strength collapses, and it is the parameter most often ruined by rain during harvest — which is why we take wheat off the field wetter and dry it, rather than waiting in the swath for a dry day.

The buyer receives the certificate before dispatch, not together with the truck. If a parameter does not suit, the lot stays on the elevator and we offer a different one. We do not ship on a "load it and we will sort it out on the weighbridge" basis.

Lot history is kept against a number: intake date, field, moisture on arrival, drying regime and the result of every analysis. If a buyer asks six months later where a consignment came from, the answer is the name of a field, not the name of a district.

  • Intake control on every truck: moisture, foreign matter, test weight.
  • Full certificate on the completed lot, issued before dispatch.
  • Lot history by number: field, drying regime, every analysis.

Storage and drying

Our own elevator holds 46,000 tonnes; with leased capacity the total is 58,000 tonnes. Drying runs at 1,200 tonnes per day across three units, so at peak harvest combines do not queue for a drier and grain does not sit in the swath under rain.

Wheat is stored separated by class and by field of origin. That costs more to administer than one large silo, and it lets a buyer assemble a required tonnage out of homogeneous lots instead of averaging everything down to the weakest figure. For repeat contracts it means the second truck matches the first.

The driers run on gas and on our own power supply with standby capacity, so a grid outage does not push the intake schedule back by a day. Through the 2026 season the elevator lost no intake days on wheat.

  • 46,000 t own storage, 58,000 t including leased capacity.
  • 1,200 t/day drying across three units.
  • Segregated storage by class and by field of origin.

Logistics and dispatch

The elevator stands in Soroka, Vinnytsia region: 35 km to Haisyn, 85 km to Vinnytsia, 230 km to Kyiv and 380 km to the port of Odesa. The port leg is a single run, with no transhipment through an intermediate warehouse.

Dispatch is by road, on your trucks or ours. A lot from 100 tonnes is scheduled for loading within three working days of agreement. Weighbridges are calibrated and certified, and weighing takes place in the presence of the buyer's representative if they wish to attend.

Our own fleet covers the peak of the season, when hired haulage doubles in price and disappears in the week it is most needed. For a buyer that means the dispatch schedule we signed does not depend on what a truck costs on the spot market that morning.

  • 35 km to Haisyn, 85 km to Vinnytsia, 230 km to Kyiv, 380 km to the port.
  • The port leg is a single run, with no intermediate warehouse.
  • Own fleet through the peak, hired trucks held as reserve.

Commercial terms

We work under contract, VAT registered, with a complete document package. Minimum lot is 100 tonnes. Spot payment is 3–5 banking days after acceptance; on a forward, an advance and a payment schedule are fixed in the contract before sowing.

Spot price is the price on the day of dispatch. A forward makes sense when the tonnage is needed in a specific month: we commit the area to you, and you get a predictable volume and a fixed price formula. Since 2008 we have shipped grain to buyers in 14 countries, and in every contract the paperwork is prepared by our own accounts department rather than an intermediary.

The question we are asked most often is whether a smaller tonnage is possible. We do not go below 100 tonnes, and not out of stubbornness: a smaller lot does not cover the cost of its own certificate, weighing and paperwork, and saying so plainly is fairer than quietly raising the price per tonne.
