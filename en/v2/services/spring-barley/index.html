Spring barley

Spring barley for feed and malting, grown on 600 hectares of our own land. The 2026 season averaged 5.2 t/ha — 3,120 tonnes, split on the elevator not by a single figure but by buyer: brewery or feed mill.

What is actually on offer

Spring barley of a single origin serves two buyers with opposite requirements. Breweries want low protein, 9.5 to 11.5%, high germinative energy and minimal pre-harvest sprouting — they need a live, vigorous kernel for malting. Feed mills take the opposite: they are happy with higher-protein lots and do not care about germinative energy, because the grain is going to a hammer mill, not a steeping tank.

This is not two different crops or two fields under different agronomy — it is one crop on 600 hectares that we split into two commercial streams at the elevator. The malting share of the harvest is not fixed as a percentage in advance: however many lots meet malting specification is how much goes to a maltster, and the rest goes to feed.

  • Malting lot — protein 9.5–11.5%, germinative energy from 95%, well-graded kernel.
  • Feed lot — the remainder of the crop, whether on protein or on sprouted-grain count.
  • The split happens at intake, not by re-screening or blending a finished lot.

Sorting at intake: where the specification is decided

Malting selection happens on the elevator at the moment of intake, not later by re-screening or blending. Every truck off the field goes through intake control: protein, moisture, sprouted-grain content, and grading on a 2.5 mm sieve. A truck that falls inside the malting window is routed to a dedicated storage section; everything else goes to feed.

We also check germinative energy — a figure a feed buyer never asks for, but one no maltster will accept a lot without. Grain with low germinative energy, even at the right protein level, is routed to feed automatically: it is not fit for malting regardless of its other numbers.

We do not try to bring a feed-grade lot up to malting specification by blending in better grain — that produces an unstable result and undermines confidence in both lots. Each lot stays exactly as it arrived from the field and passed control.

  • Intake control on every truck: protein, moisture, sprouted grains, grading.
  • Germinative energy checked separately for every potentially malting lot.
  • Lots are never blended into one another to improve a figure.

Agronomy behind the barley

Spring barley follows row crops in our rotation — soybean or maize, which leave a weed-clean, well-structured field for early spring sowing. Early sowing is critical for spring barley: the sooner the seed goes into sufficiently warmed, moist soil, the higher both germinative energy and kernel uniformity turn out.

We keep nitrogen input moderate and split across applications, because excess nitrogen raises grain protein directly, and it is high protein that disqualifies a lot from the malting stream. On fields we are targeting for malting barley, we deliberately do not push yield with nitrogen — we aim at the quality parameter instead.

Fungicide protection against ear and leaf disease produces a clean, undamaged kernel, and that is what carries high germinative energy through to harvest. Treatments are scheduled by our agronomist on scouting results, and each decision is logged with its date.

Laboratory and lot certificate

Analysis is carried out in our own laboratory at the elevator. Every completed lot gets its own certificate: test weight, protein, moisture, sprouted-grain content, grading on a 2.5 mm sieve, and foreign matter. For lots declared as malting grade, the certificate additionally reports germinative energy and nitrogen content on a dry-matter basis.

We hold test weight at 620 g/l and above — the threshold below which the coarse-fraction yield suffers on both malting and grinding. A lot whose test weight drops below that figure due to conditions during ripening is classified as feed grade automatically, regardless of its protein level.

The buyer receives the certificate before dispatch. We do not offer a maltster a lot "to try" without a completed laboratory result in hand — a purchase decision on malting barley is made on numbers, not on a visual inspection of the grain.

Storage and drying

Our own elevator holds 46,000 tonnes; with leased capacity the total is 58,000 tonnes, with drying at 1,200 tonnes per day across three units. Spring barley on 600 hectares is a small crop next to wheat or maize, so we have spare capacity to take it in and dry it quickly, without a queue.

Malting and feed lots are held in separate, clearly marked sections of the elevator to rule out accidental mixing during handling. Drying runs on a gentle temperature regime — excessive drying temperature kills germinative energy faster than any other factor, so for potentially malting lots we hold the heat-carrier temperature lower than we do for feed grain.

  • 46,000 t own storage, 58,000 t including leased capacity.
  • Separate sections for malting and feed lots.
  • Gentle drying regime to preserve germinative energy.

Commercial terms

We work under contract, VAT registered, with a complete document package. Minimum lot is 100 tonnes. Spot payment is 3–5 banking days after acceptance; on a forward we fix the tonnage and a payment schedule in the contract before sowing, which matters particularly for the malting stream, where a buyer needs volume certainty well ahead of harvest.

For a malting buyer we share an indicative volume of qualifying lots as the harvest comes in, based on preliminary intake results, and firm up the figure once the laboratory closes out the last certificates. Since 2008 we have shipped grain to buyers in 14 countries; feed lots go mostly to the domestic market, while malting lots move both domestically and on export, graded against DSTU 3769:2019 and against EBC malting barley criteria familiar to European maltsters.
